\section{Solving the tree: the lowest common ancestor}
\label{sec:lca}

\subsection{How the tree locates the route}

\begin{proposition}[Separation]
\label{prop:separation}
Let $R$ be a division with children $R_\ell,R_r$ and door $(u,v)$, $u\in R_\ell$,
$v\in R_r$. For cells $a,b$ in $R$, the route from $a$ to $b$ crosses $(u,v)$
if and only if $a$ and $b$ lie in different children.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$(u,v)$ is the only edge between the two halves (Proposition~\ref{prop:tree}).
If $a,b$ are on different sides, any route between them uses it. If they are on
the same side, that half's own spanning tree joins them, and the maze's unique
route is that one; it never leaves the half.
\end{proof}

\input{figure-separation.tex}

Deciding which side a cell is on is a comparison of its coordinates against a
rectangle. So the search descends from the root while both cells stay on one
side: the first node that separates them is their \keyterm{lowest common
ancestor}, and its door is on the route. The route is then $a\to u$, the door,
$v\to b$ --- two smaller instances of the same problem, one per half.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:lca} writes exactly that.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{The route from $a$ to $b$, both inside the tree node $R$.}
\label{alg:lca}
\SetKwFunction{Side}{Side}
\SetKwFunction{LCA}{LCA}
\SetKwFunction{Route}{Route}
\Fn{\Side{$R$, $c$}}{
  \lIf{$c$ \textup{lies in the rectangle of} $R_\ell$}{\Return \textup{\textsc{left}}}
  \Return \textup{\textsc{right}}\tcp*{two comparisons}
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\LCA{$R$, $a$, $b$}}{
  \While{$R$ \textup{is not a corridor} \textup{\textbf{and}} \Side{$R$, $a$} $=$ \Side{$R$, $b$}}{
    $R \leftarrow$ that child of $R$\;
  }
  \Return $R$\tcp*{one descent: $O(\text{depth})$}
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\Route{$R$, $a$, $b$}}{
  $R \leftarrow$ \LCA{$R$, $a$, $b$}\;
  \lIf{$R$ \textup{is a corridor}}{\Return the straight run $a\to b$}
  $(u,v)\leftarrow$ door of $R$, $u\in R_\ell$, $v\in R_r$\;
  \lIf{\Side{$R$, $a$} $=$ \textup{\textsc{left}}}{%
    \Return \Route{$R_\ell$, $a$, $u$} $\cdot\,(u\!\to\!v)\,\cdot$ \Route{$R_r$, $v$, $b$}}
  \Return \Route{$R_r$, $a$, $v$} $\cdot\,(v\!\to\!u)\,\cdot$ \Route{$R_\ell$, $u$, $b$}\;
}
\end{algorithm}

The call is \Route{root, start, exit}. \LCA{} is the $\Theta(\log n)$ walk and
finds one door; \Route{} calls it once per door on the route, and each call
after the first starts from a new cell.

No cell of the maze is read. A corridor leaf returns its whole run in one step.

\subsection{Time complexity}

Two quantities, and they must be kept apart.

\keyterm{Locating the first door} is one descent to the lowest common ancestor:
$\Theta(\text{depth})$. The tree is $\Theta(\log n)$ deep in expectation ---
measured $2\log_2 n$ --- so this is $\Theta(\log n)$.

\keyterm{Producing the whole route} is not one descent. The route crosses
$k$ division walls; each is a distinct node of the tree, and each is found by
its own descent, starting from the door the previous one found. One lowest
common ancestor finds one wall. So the cost is at least $k$, and $k$ is not
small:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrrr@{}}
  \toprule
  $n$ & $256$ & $1024$ & $4096$ & $16384$ & $65536$ \\
  \midrule
  $\log_2 n$     & $8$  & $10$ & $12$  & $14$  & $16$ \\
  walls crossed $k$ & $21$ & $44$ & $111$ & $265$ & $600$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$k$ grows as about $n^{0.57}$. The tree path from the start's leaf up to the
common ancestor and down to the exit's leaf --- the walk that costs
$\Theta(\log n)$ --- has $18$ nodes at $256\times256$ and holds $12$ of the
route's $613$ doors; the other $601$ sit on nodes that walk never visits, because
each door found lands on a new cell whose common ancestor with the exit is a new
node. Exactly, the cost is the number of tree nodes whose region the route
enters. Writing $p$ for the fraction of descents at
which the two endpoints are separated,
\[
  T(m)=(1+p)\,T(m/2)+O(1)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  T(n)=\Theta\!\left(n^{\log_2(1+p)}\right),
\]
and the measured $p\approx0.61$ gives $\Theta(n^{0.68})$. $p$ only sets the
exponent; the bound $\Omega(k)$ above needs no parameter at all, and neither
does $\Omega(d)$, the length of the route, which is the output.

That no algorithm does better from the tree can be seen directly. Build two
mazes that agree on the top $\log_2 n$ levels of the tree and differ only below:
in $20$ pairs at $256\times256$, the routes differed in every pair. An algorithm
reading only $\log n$ nodes reads the same nodes in both mazes and must give the
same answer, so it is wrong on one of them. \keyterm{The route depends on nodes
a logarithmic walk never reads.} What $\Theta(\log n)$ does buy is the first
wall, and the report claims no more.

\begin{keypoint}
$\Theta(\log n)$ to find where the route crosses the first wall;
$\Theta(n^{0.68})$ to lay the whole route down. Both are far below A*'s
$\Theta(n)$ settled cells.
\end{keypoint}
