\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem-formulation}

\subsection{Statement}

A rectangular area of $W\times H$ unit cells is to be laid out as a maze. The
layout must be drawn at random, so that $W$ and $H$ do not determine it; the
area has a single exit on its boundary; and it is entered at an arbitrary
interior cell. Two problems follow, and both are to be settled algorithmically
before any implementation and then analysed for cost:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
  \item given the width and the height of the initial area, generate a maze;
  \item find the path from any position in the maze to the exit, which the
        specification states is unique and hence shortest.
\end{enumerate}

The construction is prescribed. Divide the allocated space with a full wall
across its longest dimension, leave exactly one door in that wall, and apply
the same rule to each of the two spaces this creates.
Figure~\ref{fig:construction-rule} is the entire rule; everything the report
derives follows from it.

\subsection{From a drawing of walls to a graph}

A maze drawn on paper is a picture of walls; what an algorithm can generate,
store and search is a graph. The translation is the first decision of this
report, and every later section rests on it.

\begin{definition}[Area, cells, grid graph]
Let $W,H\in\mathbb{N}^{*}$ be the width and the height of the allocated area,
measured in unit cells. The cells are
\[
  \cells=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\;:\;0\le x<W,\;0\le y<H\},
  \qquad |\cells|=WH,
\]
and two cells are \emph{adjacent} when they share a side, that is when
$|x-x'|+|y-y'|=1$. The \emph{grid graph} $\gridgraph=(\cells,\gridedges)$ has
the cells as vertices and the adjacent pairs as edges, so
\[
  |\gridedges| \;=\; \underbrace{H(W-1)}_{\text{horizontal}}
                  +\underbrace{W(H-1)}_{\text{vertical}}
               \;=\; 2WH-W-H .
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Maze]
A \emph{maze} on that area is a spanning subgraph $\maze=(\cells,E)$ with
$E\subseteq\gridedges$. An edge of $E$ is a \emph{door}: the two cells it joins
can be walked between. A pair of $\gridedges\setminus E$ is a \emph{wall
segment}. One distinguished boundary cell $\exitcell$ is the exit.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{fig:maze-and-its-graph} puts the two readings of the same object
side by side. The vertex set is fixed by $W$ and $H$ alone; generating a maze
therefore means nothing more than \emph{choosing which subset of
$\gridedges$ to keep}.

\subsection{What the specification forces \texorpdfstring{$E$}{E} to be}

Three requirements follow from the statement.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(R\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Every cell reaches the exit.} The entry position is arbitrary,
        so any cell may be a starting point and each must have a route out:
        $\maze$ is connected.
  \item \textbf{There is exactly one exit.} $\exitcell$ is a single cell on the
        boundary of the area, and the outer wall is closed everywhere else.
  \item \textbf{The route out does not fork.} The path to the exit is required
        to be unique, hence shortest; a cycle in $\maze$ would give two
        distinct simple routes between the cells it passes through, so $\maze$
        is acyclic.
\end{enumerate}

Connected and acyclic on a fixed vertex set is a spanning tree, which settles
the shape of the answer before any algorithm is written.

\begin{proposition}[The object to produce]
\label{prop:spanning-tree}
A subgraph $\maze=(\cells,E)$ of $\gridgraph$ satisfies \textup{(R1)} and
\textup{(R3)} if and only if it is a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$. It then has
$|E|=WH-1$ doors, and between any two cells there is exactly one simple path,
which is therefore also a shortest one.
\end{proposition}

That last clause is what makes the second problem well posed: asking for
\emph{the} path out is only meaningful because there is one. It is also the
lever for the rest of the report --- Section~\ref{sec:maze-generation} shows
that the construction rule produces such a tree \emph{by construction} rather
than by checking afterwards.

\subsection{The two problems, restated on the graph}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
  \item[Problem 1 --- Generation.] Given $W$, $H$, an exit cell $\exitcell$ and
    a source of random bits, produce a spanning tree $\maze$ of $\gridgraph$,
    drawn at random so that no two runs need agree, together with the
    structure that records how it was built.
  \item[Problem 2 --- Escape.] Given $\maze$ and any start cell
    $s\in\cells$, return the unique simple path from $s$ to $\exitcell$.
\end{description}

Problem~1 is solved in Section~\ref{sec:maze-generation}. Problem~2 needs a
notion of length before it can be measured: Section~\ref{sec:unit-edge-weights}
gives every door weight $1$, and Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} runs the
shortest-path search that weight makes meaningful, guided by the Manhattan and
Euclidean distances.

\subsection{Conventions}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
  \toprule
  Symbol & Meaning \\
  \midrule
  $W$, $H$            & width and height of the area, in cells \\
  $n=WH$              & number of cells, the size of the input \\
  $(x,y)$             & cell in column $x$ (left to right) and row $y$ (top to bottom) \\
  $\gridgraph$, $\gridedges$ & the grid graph and its edges: every adjacency, all doors open \\
  $\maze=(\cells,E)$  & the maze produced: a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$ \\
  $\exitcell$         & the single exit cell, on the boundary \\
  $\region{a_0,a_1}{b_0,b_1}$ & a region: columns $a_0..a_1$, rows $b_0..b_1$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
