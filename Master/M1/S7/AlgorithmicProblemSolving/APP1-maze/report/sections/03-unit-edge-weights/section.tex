% Section 3: the cost model. One door, one step -- which is what turns "the way
% out" into a quantity, and what makes the search of Section 4 applicable.
\section{Encoding the distance between adjacent cells as \texorpdfstring{$1$}{1}}
\label{sec:unit-edge-weights}

\subsection{One door, one step}

Section~\ref{sec:maze-generation} produced a graph. It has no lengths on it yet,
so ``the shortest way out'' is not a question it can answer.

The cost model is the simplest one the problem admits.

\begin{keypoint}
Walking from a room to an adjacent room through a \doorword{door} is
\keyterm{one step}. Every edge of $\maze$ carries weight $w(e)=1$.
\end{keypoint}

The length of a route is then the \keyterm{number of doors it crosses}, and
nothing else. A route through $k+1$ cells costs $k$.

\begin{definition}[Distance in the maze]
For cells $u,v$, let $d(u,v)$ be the least total weight of a route from $u$ to
$v$ in $\maze$. Under unit weights this is the number of doors on that route.
\end{definition}

\subsection{What the model buys}

Three things follow immediately, and together they are why the next section can
be written at all.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline,itemsep=5pt]
  \item[The weights are non-negative.] That is the one precondition Dijkstra's
    algorithm asks of a graph~\cite{dijkstra1959note}. So the search of
    Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} applies to $\maze$ without further argument.
  \item[Every cell is a vertex; every door is an edge.] The maze is already the
    graph the search runs on. No transformation, no auxiliary structure: the
    node records of Section~\ref{sec:maze-generation} are the adjacency list.
  \item[Distance is depth.] With all weights equal, $d(s,\cdot)$ counts doors,
    so the cells at distance $k$ from $s$ form a layer. This is the observation
    Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} turns on.
\end{description}

\subsection{What the model forbids}

Stating the model's limits is part of stating the model.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \keyterm{No weighted doors.} A door that is slower to pass --- a
        staircase, a flooded corridor --- cannot be expressed. The graph would
        stay the same and only $w$ would change, so the search would still
        work; but every count in Section~\ref{sec:analysis} is a count of
        \emph{doors}, and would stop meaning \emph{time}.
  \item \keyterm{No diagonal movement.} Cells are adjacent only across a shared
        side, so the grid is $4$-connected. This is what makes the Manhattan
        distance the natural estimate in Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path}; on an
        $8$-connected grid the right estimate would be the octile distance
        instead.
  \item \keyterm{No cost for turning.} A route that zigzags and a route that
        runs straight cost the same if they cross the same number of doors.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the shortest route is not in doubt}

On a general graph, a unit-weight shortest path is one of possibly many. Here it
is not.

By Proposition~\ref{prop:spanning-tree} the maze is a spanning tree, so between
any two cells there is exactly one simple route. That route is therefore
trivially the shortest, whatever weights are used.

\begin{keypoint}
The search of Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} is not choosing between routes.
There is only one. What it does is \keyterm{locate} it, and the only thing that
separates one search from another is \keyterm{how much of the maze it looks at
on the way}.
\end{keypoint}

That is the measure Section~\ref{sec:cpp-testing} reports and
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} explains: not the length of the answer, which is
fixed, but the number of cells expanded before the answer is reached.
