\section{Maze generation, and the two structures it returns}
\label{sec:maze-generation}

Section~\ref{sec:problem-formulation} fixed the target: a spanning tree of
$\gridgraph$, drawn at random.

This section builds one. The structure comes first, because the algorithm is
written against it. The guarantee comes last, because the construction rule is
what supplies it.

\subsection{Two outputs, not one}

The generator fills two structures as it goes, and returns both. They are the
same maze seen twice, and neither is derived from the other.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline,itemsep=6pt]
  \item[The \keyterm{graph}: which doors exist.] A set of cells, each holding
    the neighbours it has a door to. It is what a walker needs, and what
    Section~\ref{sec:dijkstra} searches.
  \item[The \keyterm{binary tree}: how it was built.] One node per division,
    recording where the wall went and where its door was left, with the two
    sub-mazes that division created as its children. It is what
    Section~\ref{sec:common-ancestor} reads.
\end{description}

Building the second costs nothing asymptotically --- the recursion already has
the information, and only has to keep it --- and
Section~\ref{sec:common-ancestor} shows it is worth thirty times its price.

\subsection{The node record}

The maze is a set of nodes, one per cell. A node holds

\[
  \texttt{Node} \;=\; \bigl\langle\;
    \underbrace{\textit{value}}_{\text{its }(x,y)\text{ coordinates}},\;\;
    \underbrace{\textit{neighbours}}_{\text{the cells it has a door to}}
  \;\bigr\rangle .
\]

The neighbour list is \keyterm{empty when the node is created}. A cell has four
sides, so it never holds more than four entries, and it grows only when the
construction puts a \doorword{door} on that node.

An index $\langle P_i,P_j\rangle$ --- a $W\times H$ array of references --- maps
a coordinate pair to its node in $O(1)$. A division reaches the cells it wants
without searching. Figure~\ref{fig:node-record} is one node before and after.

\input{figure-node-record.tex}

Each door is recorded at both ends, so the finished maze holds $2(WH-1)$ entries
for $WH-1$ doors. That buys enumeration of a cell's exits in $O(\deg)$ from the
cell alone, which is what Section~\ref{sec:dijkstra} does at every step.

\subsection{Regions, named by two corners}
\label{sec:regions}

A region is a rectangle of cells, named by the two cells at its ends:
$\region{x_0,y_0}{x_1,y_1}$, upper-left and lower-right, with $x_0\le x_1$ and
$y_0\le y_1$. The whole area is $\region{0,0}{W-1,H-1}$.

Both corners are \keyterm{cells of the region}, not fences around it. A region
spans $x_1-x_0+1$ columns and $y_1-y_0+1$ rows.

In the pseudocode the two corners are $p$ and $q$, and a cell's coordinates are
reached by index: $p[0]$ is its $x$, $p[1]$ its $y$. Indexing rather than naming
them lets the division below be written once for both axes.

\subsection{The recursion stops at a corridor}
\label{sec:corridor-base}

\begin{definition}[Corridor]
\label{def:corridor}
A region is a \emph{corridor} when one of its two sides is a single cell:
$x_0=x_1$, one column wide and any height, or $y_0=y_1$, one row tall and any
width. A single cell, where both hold, is the smallest corridor.
\end{definition}

A corridor is the case where the construction has nothing to decide.

Its cells lie in a line, so the subgraph $\gridgraph$ induces on it is already a
path. A path on $m$ vertices has $m-1$ edges and is a tree, so it is its own and
only spanning tree.

Put a \wallword{wall} across a corridor and it has exactly one crossing, which
is then forced to be the \doorword{door}. The division would change nothing.

So the recursion stops there. \texttt{SpanCorridor} lays those forced doors and
returns the leaf recording them.

\input{figure-corridor.tex}

\begin{keypoint}
Stopping at a corridor means \keyterm{every region reaching a division has both
sides at least $2$}. Both axes always admit a wall, so the orientation is a
plain coin with no test in front of it.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{One division contributes exactly one door}
\label{sec:one-door-per-division}

Take any door $e=(u,v)$ of the finished maze and walk down the region tree from
the root.

At each internal node, $u$ and $v$ either fall in the same child --- and the
walk continues --- or they are separated. They are separated exactly when $e$
crosses that node's wall, and then $e$ is that node's door, being the only
crossing there is.

Otherwise the walk ends at a leaf holding both, and $e$ is one of the doors that
leaf laid along its corridor.

So the doors split cleanly in two: one per internal node, plus the interiors of
the corridor leaves. Nothing is counted twice, nothing is missed.

\begin{keypoint}
That licenses the construction to run \keyterm{forwards}. A division adds its
own door and nothing else; a leaf adds its corridor. A wall is never represented
at all --- it is simply the doors that were never added.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{The second output: the binary tree}

The recursion's shape is a binary tree, one node per call.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
  \item An \keyterm{internal node} records one division: its orientation $t$,
        the boundary $j$ the wall sits on, the position $i$ of its door. Its two
        children are the two regions the division created.
  \item A \keyterm{leaf} is a corridor, of one cell or many.
\end{itemize}

A binary tree with $L$ leaves has $L-1$ internal nodes. So of the $WH-1$ doors,
$L-1$ are division doors and $WH-L$ are corridor doors.

Figure~\ref{fig:region-tree} shows the first three divisions of a $4\times4$
area beside the top of the tree they build.

\subsection{One division step}

A call handles a region $\region{x_0,y_0}{x_1,y_1}$. If it is a corridor,
Section~\ref{sec:corridor-base} disposes of it. Otherwise both sides are at
least $2$, and:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
  \item \keyterm{Toss for the axis $t$ to cut.} Both admit a wall, so the coin
        is free. Write $t=0$ for the $x$ axis and $t=1$ for the $y$ axis; then
        $1-t$ is the other one.
  \item \keyterm{Draw the wall position $j$} on axis $t$, uniformly in
        $[\,p[t],\,q[t]-1\,]$, and \keyterm{the door position $i$} on the other
        axis, uniformly in $[\,p[1-t],\,q[1-t]\,]$.
  \item \keyterm{Link the two cells the door falls on}, appending each to the
        other's neighbour list.
  \item \keyterm{Recurse} on the two regions --- the same corners, cut at $j$ on
        axis $t$ --- and hang them under the node recording $\langle
        t,j,i\rangle$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Departure from the specification]
The specification puts the wall across the \emph{longest} dimension. This report
tosses for it instead. Correctness is untouched:
Proposition~\ref{prop:generation-is-a-tree} never uses which axis was cut, only
that one was.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Maze generation by recursive division.}
\label{alg:generate-maze}
\SetKwFunction{GenerateMaze}{GenerateMaze}
\SetKwFunction{Divide}{Divide}
\SetKwFunction{RandomIn}{RandomIn}
\SetKwFunction{RandomBit}{RandomBit}
\SetKwFunction{Link}{Link}
\SetKwFunction{Span}{SpanCorridor}
\SetKwFunction{Leaf}{Leaf}
\SetKwFunction{NodeOf}{CutNode}
\SetKwFunction{NewNode}{NewNode}
\SetKwFunction{CellAt}{CellAt}
\SetKwFunction{WithAxis}{WithAxis}
\KwIn{$W,H\ge1$, the width and height of the area in cells. The bound is a
      precondition, not a convention: at $W=0$ the region $\region{0,0}{-1,H-1}$
      holds no cell and there is nothing for the first draw to land on.}
\KwOut{$N$, the set of nodes holding a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$; $r$, the
       root of the region tree that built it.}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\GenerateMaze{$W$, $H$}}{
  $N \leftarrow \{\,\NewNode{$x$, $y$} : 0\le x<W,\; 0\le y<H\,\}$
     \tcp*{every neighbour list empty}
  $r \leftarrow$ \Divide{$N$, $(0,0)$, $(W-1,H-1)$}\;
  \Return $(N,r)$\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\Divide{$N$, $p$, $q$}}{
  \lIf{$p[0]=q[0]$ \textup{\textbf{or}} $p[1]=q[1]$}{\Return \Span{$N$, $p$, $q$}}
  $t \leftarrow$ \RandomBit{}\tcp*{the axis to cut: $0$ is $x$, $1$ is $y$}
  $j \leftarrow$ \RandomIn{$p[t]$, $q[t]-1$}\tcp*{wall between $j$ and $j+1$ on axis $t$}
  $i \leftarrow$ \RandomIn{$p[1{-}t]$, $q[1{-}t]$}\tcp*{the one door, at $i$ on the other axis}
  \Link{$N$, \CellAt{$t$, $j$, $i$}, \CellAt{$t$, $j{+}1$, $i$}}\;
  $\ell \leftarrow$ \Divide{$N$, $p$, \WithAxis{$q$, $t$, $j$}}\;
  $r \leftarrow$ \Divide{$N$, \WithAxis{$p$, $t$, $j{+}1$}, $q$}\;
  \Return \NodeOf{$t$, $j$, $i$, $\ell$, $r$}\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\Span{$N$, $p$, $q$}}{
  \lFor{$x \leftarrow p[0]$ \KwTo $q[0]-1$}{\Link{$N$, $(x,p[1])$, $(x{+}1,p[1])$}}
  \lFor{$y \leftarrow p[1]$ \KwTo $q[1]-1$}{\Link{$N$, $(p[0],y)$, $(p[0],y{+}1)$}}
  \Return \Leaf{$p$, $q$}\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\Link{$N$, $u$, $v$}}{
  append $N[v]$ to $N[u].\textit{neighbours}$\;
  append $N[u]$ to $N[v].\textit{neighbours}$\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\CellAt{$t$, $u$, $v$}}{
  \Return the cell whose axis-$t$ coordinate is $u$ and whose other is $v$\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\WithAxis{$p$, $t$, $u$}}{
  \Return the cell $p$ with its axis-$t$ coordinate replaced by $u$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\Span{} is the whole base case. It lays the corridor's forced doors \emph{and}
returns the leaf recording the region.

It needs no case analysis: on a corridor one of its loops is empty by
construction, on a single cell both are. The same two loops cover a row, a
column and one cell.

Every \Link{} joins two cells of the region that called it, and they are
adjacent. So every appended entry is a genuine edge of $\gridgraph$.

The exit plays no part, for the reasons in
Section~\ref{sec:exit-constraints}. The algorithm never reads it, and any cell
may be designated once the tree exists.

\subsection{Why the result is a spanning tree}

\begin{proposition}[Correctness of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generate-maze}]
\label{prop:generation-is-a-tree}
On a region of $m$ cells, \textup{\texttt{Divide}} leaves the subgraph induced
on that region connected and acyclic, with exactly $m-1$ doors. In particular
\textup{\texttt{GenerateMaze}} returns a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $m$.

\emph{Base.} If the region has a side of one it is a corridor of $m$ cells in a
line, and \texttt{SpanCorridor} joins each to the next: a path, connected and
acyclic, carrying $m-1$ doors. This covers $m=1$, where both loops are empty and
$m-1=0$.

\emph{Step.} Otherwise both sides are at least $2$, so $q[0]-p[0]\ge1$ and
$q[1]-p[1]\ge1$. Whichever axis the coin names, $q[t]-p[t]\ge1$, so $j$ is drawn
from a non-empty range and the two sub-regions $R_\ell$ and $R_r$ are non-empty,
with $m_\ell+m_r=m$.

The call adds the single door at $i$, whose two cells lie one in each
sub-region, then recurses. By the induction hypothesis the two calls leave their
regions connected and acyclic with $m_\ell-1$ and $m_r-1$ doors. Neither can add
an edge between the sub-regions, since every \texttt{Link} a call makes joins
two cells of its own region.

So the door at $i$ is the only edge between $R_\ell$ and $R_r$. Joining two
disjoint trees by a single edge yields a tree. It is connected, since any two
cells are joined inside their own half or by crossing that door. It is acyclic,
since a cycle would have to cross the wall an even, hence non-zero, number of
times while only one crossing exists. The door count is
$(m_\ell-1)+(m_r-1)+1=m-1$.

Applied to the whole area this gives a connected acyclic spanning subgraph of
$\gridgraph$ on all $WH$ cells: a spanning tree, with $WH-1$ doors.
\end{proof}

The guarantee is structural. Nothing is checked after the fact and no attempt is
ever rejected and retried.

The uniqueness of the route out --- the whole of Problem~2 --- follows from the
shape of the recursion, not from a property that had to be tested.

\subsection{What generation returns}

Generation returns both structures: the set $N$ of nodes, each carrying its
coordinates and the doors it ended up with, and the root $r$ of the binary
tree.

  Output & What it holds & Solved by \\
  \midrule
  Graph & each node's neighbours and coordinates
        & Dijkstra, Section~\ref{sec:dijkstra} \\
  Binary tree & one node per division, with its door
        & common ancestor, Section~\ref{sec:common-ancestor} \\

The coordinates in the first row are what make the heuristics of
Section~\ref{sec:dijkstra} possible: an estimate of the remaining distance
needs to know where the cell is.
