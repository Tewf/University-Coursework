\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem-formulation}

\subsection{Statement}

A rectangular area of $W\times H$ unit cells is to be laid out as a maze.

Three things are fixed. The layout is drawn \keyterm{at random}, so $W$ and $H$
do not determine it. The area is \keyterm{enclosed} and has a single
\keyterm{exit}. It is entered at an arbitrary cell.

Two problems follow. Both are settled on paper first, then analysed for cost.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item Given the width and the height of the initial area, generate a maze.
  \item Find the path from any position in the maze to the exit. The
        specification states it is unique, hence shortest.
\end{enumerate}

The construction is prescribed.

\begin{keypoint}
Divide the allocated space with a full \wallword{wall} across its longest
dimension. Leave exactly one \doorword{door} in that wall. Apply the same rule
to each of the two spaces this creates.
\end{keypoint}

Figure~\ref{fig:construction-rule} is the entire rule. Everything below follows
from it.

\input{figure-construction-rule.tex}

\paragraph{What ``at random'' binds.}
Half the output, not all of it.

The cells are fixed by $W$ and $H$. The same $WH$ positions appear in every run,
and their coordinates are public the moment the dimensions are.

What must be unpredictable is \keyterm{which doors were kept} --- the edge set.

So the requirement falls on the draws, not on the storage. A rule that takes the
wall from the geometry, the midpoint say, makes the maze a function of $W$ and
$H$. Anyone holding those two numbers could rebuild it.

\subsection{From a drawing of walls to a graph}

A maze on paper is a picture of walls. What an algorithm can generate, store and
search is a graph.

That translation is the first decision of this report, and every later section
rests on it.

\begin{definition}[Area, cells, grid graph]
Let $W,H\in\mathbb{N}^{*}$ be the width and the height of the area, in unit
cells. The cells are
\[
  \cells=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\;:\;0\le x<W,\;0\le y<H\},
  \qquad |\cells|=WH,
\]
and two cells are \emph{adjacent} when they share a side, that is when
$|x-x'|+|y-y'|=1$. The \emph{grid graph} $\gridgraph=(\cells,\gridedges)$ has
the cells as vertices and the adjacent pairs as edges, so
\[
  |\gridedges| \;=\; \underbrace{H(W-1)}_{\text{horizontal}}
                  +\underbrace{W(H-1)}_{\text{vertical}}
               \;=\; 2WH-W-H .
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Maze]
A \emph{maze} on that area is a spanning subgraph $\maze=(\cells,E)$ with
$E\subseteq\gridedges$. An edge of $E$ is a \doorword{door}: the two cells it
joins can be walked between. A pair of $\gridedges\setminus E$ is a
\wallword{wall segment}. One distinguished cell $\exitcell$ is the exit.
\end{definition}

Figure~\ref{fig:maze-and-its-graph} shows the two readings of one object.

The vertex set is fixed by $W$ and $H$ alone. So generating a maze means nothing
more than \keyterm{choosing which subset of $\gridedges$ to keep}.

\subsection{What the specification forces \texorpdfstring{$E$}{E} to be}

Three requirements follow from the statement.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(R\arabic*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
  \item \keyterm{Every cell reaches the exit.} The entry position is arbitrary,
        so any cell may be a start and each must have a route out. $\maze$ is
        connected.
  \item \keyterm{There is exactly one exit.} One cell is designated
        $\exitcell$. It is a \emph{cell}, not a gap in the outer wall: the area
        is enclosed on every side, and the exit is the one place leading out of
        it. It may lie anywhere, including strictly inside.
  \item \keyterm{The route out does not fork.} The path to the exit is required
        to be unique, hence shortest. A cycle in $\maze$ would give two distinct
        simple routes between the cells it passes through, so $\maze$ is
        acyclic.
\end{enumerate}

Connected and acyclic on a fixed vertex set is a spanning tree. The shape of the
answer is settled before any algorithm is written.

\begin{proposition}[The object to produce]
\label{prop:spanning-tree}
A subgraph $\maze=(\cells,E)$ of $\gridgraph$ satisfies \textup{(R1)} and
\textup{(R3)} if and only if it is a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$. It then has
$|E|=WH-1$ doors, and between any two cells there is exactly one simple path,
which is therefore also a shortest one.
\end{proposition}

That last clause is what makes Problem~2 well posed. Asking for \emph{the} path
out is only meaningful because there is one.

It is also the lever for the rest of the report:
Section~\ref{sec:maze-generation} produces such a tree \keyterm{by
construction}, rather than by checking afterwards.

\subsection{Constraints on the exit}
\label{sec:exit-constraints}

Three questions about $\exitcell$, none of which constrains the construction.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline,itemsep=5pt]
  \item[Chosen before or after generation?] After. Generation takes $W$ and $H$
    and nothing else, and no step of it could consult an exit. By
    Proposition~\ref{prop:spanning-tree} every cell is reachable from every
    other, so any cell designated afterwards works.
  \item[On the boundary?] No. The exit is a cell of the area, not a hole in the
    wall enclosing it, so nothing obliges it to touch the perimeter.
  \item[Exactly one door?] No. Uniqueness of the route out comes from $\maze$
    being a tree, not from the exit's degree: with $1$, $2$, $3$ or $4$ doors
    there is still exactly one simple path to it from anywhere.
\end{description}

\begin{keypoint}
The exit carries no constraint. It is a \keyterm{label applied to a finished
tree}, and every cell is an equally valid candidate.
Figure~\ref{fig:maze-and-its-graph} marks an interior one of degree $3$.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{The two problems, restated on the graph}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline,itemsep=5pt]
  \item[Problem 1 --- Generation.] Given $W$, $H$ and a source of random bits,
    produce a spanning tree $\maze$ of $\gridgraph$, drawn so that no two runs
    need agree, together with the structure recording how it was built. The
    exit is not an input.
  \item[Problem 2 --- Escape.] Given $\maze$ and any start cell $s\in\cells$,
    return the unique simple path from $s$ to $\exitcell$.
\end{description}

Problem~1 is solved in Section~\ref{sec:maze-generation}.

Problem~2 needs a notion of length first. Section~\ref{sec:unit-edge-weights}
gives every door weight $1$; Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} runs the
shortest-path search that weight makes meaningful, guided by the Manhattan and
Euclidean distances.

\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
  \toprule
  Symbol & Meaning \\
  \midrule
  $W$, $H$            & width and height of the area, in cells \\
  $n=WH$              & number of cells, the size of the input \\
  $(x,y)$             & cell in column $x$ (left to right), row $y$ (top to bottom) \\
  $\gridgraph$, $\gridedges$ & the grid graph and its edges: every adjacency \\
  $\maze=(\cells,E)$  & the maze produced: a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$ \\
