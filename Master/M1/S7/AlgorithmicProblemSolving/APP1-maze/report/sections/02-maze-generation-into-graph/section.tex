% comes out is the spanning tree Section 1 said it had to be. Two decisions

\section{Generating the maze into a graph}
\label{sec:maze-generation}

Section~\ref{sec:problem-formulation} fixed the target: a spanning tree of
$\gridgraph$, drawn at random. This section builds one. The structure comes
first, because the algorithm is written against it, and the guarantee comes
last, because it is the construction rule that supplies it.

\subsection{What has to be stored}

Two different things are worth keeping, and confusing them is the usual way to
end up storing neither well.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
  \item[The maze itself --- which doors exist.] It is what a walker needs, and
    the only thing needed to answer Problem~2.
  \item[The way it was built --- the region decomposition.] Which wall was put
    where, and in what order. It is the binary tree the divide-and-conquer rule
    produces for free, and the object whose depth the cost analysis of
    Section~\ref{sec:analysis} is written in terms of.
\end{description}

\subsection{The node record}

The maze is a set of nodes, one per cell. A node holds

\[
  \texttt{Node} \;=\; \bigl\langle\;
    \underbrace{\textit{value}}_{\text{its }(x,y)\text{ coordinates}},\;\;
    \underbrace{\textit{neighbours}}_{\text{the cells it has a door to}}
  \;\bigr\rangle,
\]

and the neighbour list is \emph{empty when the node is created}. A cell has
four sides, so the list never holds more than four entries, and it grows only
when the construction puts a door on that node. An index $\langle
P_i,P_j\rangle$ --- a $W\times H$ array of references --- maps a coordinate pair
to its node in $O(1)$, so a division can reach the cells it wants without
searching. Figure~\ref{fig:node-record} is one node before and after.

\input{figure-node-record.tex}

Each door is recorded at both of its ends, so the finished maze holds
$2(WH-1)$ list entries for $WH-1$ doors. That is the cost of being able to
enumerate a cell's exits in $O(\deg)$ from the cell alone, which is what the
search of Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} does at every step.

\begin{remark}
An alternative is to give each cell two links, to its right and lower
neighbour, so that every \emph{possible} door is owned by exactly one cell and
none is stored twice. That trick exists to stop a dense grid of candidate edges
being duplicated. It has nothing to save here: the construction below never
represents a door that does not exist, so there is no duplication to avoid and
no \textsc{nil} to store for a wall.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Storing the coordinates costs nothing in secrecy. The cells and their positions
are fixed by $W$ and $H$, and are known to anyone who knows the dimensions; what
the randomness has to protect is the edge set, and a node's \textit{value}
reveals nothing about which of its four sides became doors. The list does, but
the list \emph{is} the maze.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Regions, named by two corners}
\label{sec:regions}

A region is a rectangle of cells, and the algorithm names it by the two cells
at its ends: $\region{x_0,y_0}{x_1,y_1}$, upper-left and lower-right, with
$x_0\le x_1$ and $y_0\le y_1$. The whole area is $\region{0,0}{W-1,H-1}$. In the
pseudocode those two cells are called $p$ and $q$, and a cell's two coordinates
are reached by index: $p[0]$ is its $x$ and $p[1]$ its $y$. Indexing rather than
naming them is what lets the division below be written once for both axes.

This is the same currency the rest of the program is written in. A node's
\textit{value} is an $(x,y)$ pair, a door is a pair of them, and now a region is
too, so there is one coordinate type throughout and no second convention to
keep straight --- which is also how the drawing routine provided with the
subject speaks, taking a rectangle as two corner points.

Both corners are \emph{cells of the region}, not fences around it. A region
therefore spans $x_1-x_0+1$ columns and $y_1-y_0+1$ rows, and one column wide is
$x_0=x_1$ --- a difference of \emph{zero}. A difference of one, $x_1-x_0=1$, is a
region two columns wide, which is not a corridor and still has a wall to place.
Naming a region by a half-open range instead, with $q$ one cell past the last,
would make the width $x_1-x_0$ and one-wide $x_1-x_0=1$; it is rejected because
$q$ would then not be a cell, which puts back exactly the second convention the
corner form removed.

\subsection{The recursion stops at a corridor}
\label{sec:corridor-base}

\begin{definition}[Corridor]
\label{def:corridor}
A region is a \emph{corridor} when one of its two sides is a single cell:
$x_0=x_1$, one column wide and any height, or $y_0=y_1$, one row tall and any
width. A single cell, where both hold, is the smallest corridor.
\end{definition}

The name is worth the trouble because a corridor is the case where the
construction has nothing to decide. Its cells lie in a line, so the subgraph
$\gridgraph$ induces on it is already a path; a path on $m$ vertices has $m-1$
edges and is a tree, so it is its own and only spanning tree. Put a wall across
a corridor and it has exactly one crossing, which is then forced to be the
door --- the division would change nothing. Dividing further is therefore not
wrong, merely pointless, and the recursion stops there, handing the region to
\texttt{SpanCorridor}, which lays those forced doors and returns the leaf that
records them.

\input{figure-corridor.tex}

Stopping at a corridor rather than at a single cell is not only economy, though
it is substantial economy: on a $64\times64$ area it took $1301$ calls against
$8191$, and a $1\times40$ area is finished in a single call instead of $79$. The
real payoff is that \emph{every region reaching a division now has both sides at
least $2$}. Both axes therefore admit a wall, always, and the orientation can be
a plain coin with no feasibility test in front of it. The degenerate case is not
guarded against; it is removed.

\subsection{One division contributes exactly one door}
\label{sec:one-door-per-division}

Take any door $e=(u,v)$ of the finished maze and walk down the region tree from
the root. At each internal node, $u$ and $v$ either fall in the same child ---
and the walk continues --- or they are separated, which happens precisely when
$e$ crosses that node's wall, and then $e$ is that node's door, being the only
crossing that survived. Otherwise the walk ends at a leaf holding both, and $e$
is one of the doors that leaf laid down along its corridor.

So the doors split cleanly in two: one per internal node, plus the internal
doors of each corridor leaf. Nothing is counted twice and nothing is missed,
which is what licenses the construction to run \emph{forwards} --- a division
adds its own door and nothing else, a leaf adds its corridor, and a wall is
never represented at all. It is simply the doors that were never added.

\begin{remark}
This is what makes the neighbour list a sound representation. A list cannot
express ``these two cells are not joined'' except by omission, which is useless
if the algorithm needs to remove links; here nothing is ever removed, so
omission is exactly right.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The region tree}

The recursion's shape is a binary tree, one node per call:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item an \textbf{internal node} records one division --- its orientation $t$,
        the boundary $j$ the wall sits on, the position $i$ of its single door
        --- and has the two regions the division created as its children;
  \item a \textbf{leaf} is a corridor, of one cell or many.
\end{itemize}

A binary tree with $L$ leaves has $L-1$ internal nodes, so the $WH-1$ doors are
$L-1$ division doors and $WH-L$ corridor doors. Figure~\ref{fig:region-tree}
shows the first three divisions of a $4\times4$ area beside the top of the tree
they build.

\subsection{One division step}

A call handles a region $\region{x_0,y_0}{x_1,y_1}$. If it is a corridor
(Definition~\ref{def:corridor}) Section~\ref{sec:corridor-base} disposes of it.
Otherwise both sides are at least $2$ and:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Toss for the axis $t$ to cut.} Both admit a wall, so the coin is
        free. Write $t=0$ for the $x$ axis and $t=1$ for the $y$ axis, and write
        $p[t]$ for the coordinate of cell $p$ on axis $t$; then $1-t$ is the
        other axis, and one piece of code covers both cases.
  \item \textbf{Choose the wall position $j$} on axis $t$, uniformly in
        $[\,p[t],\,q[t]-1\,]$, and \textbf{the door position $i$} on the other
        axis, uniformly in $[\,p[1-t],\,q[1-t]\,]$.
  \item \textbf{Link the two cells the door falls on}, appending each to the
        other's neighbour list.
  \item \textbf{Recurse} on the two regions --- the same corners with the
        axis-$t$ coordinate cut at $j$ --- and hang them under the node
        recording $\langle t,j,i\rangle$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Why one branch and not two]
Written out longhand, the vertical case and the horizontal case are the same
six lines with $x$ and $y$ exchanged. Indexing the axis collapses them into one,
which is worth doing for a reason beyond brevity: two mirrored copies are where
a transcription slip hides, since each reads correctly on its own and only the
comparison between them shows the error. One copy has nothing to disagree with.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Departure from the specification]
The specification puts the wall across the \emph{longest} dimension; this
report tosses for it, which is a deliberate deviation and worth naming. The
longest-dimension rule keeps regions square, so it is the more disciplined
choice; a free coin lets regions grow long and thin and makes the maze more
corridor-like. It costs little in depth, because depth is driven by the uniform
\emph{position} of the wall rather than by its orientation, and correctness is
untouched: Proposition~\ref{prop:generation-is-a-tree} never uses which axis was
cut, only that one was.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Maze generation by recursive division.}
\label{alg:generate-maze}
\SetKwFunction{GenerateMaze}{GenerateMaze}
\SetKwFunction{Divide}{Divide}
\SetKwFunction{RandomIn}{RandomIn}
\SetKwFunction{RandomBit}{RandomBit}
\SetKwFunction{Link}{Link}
\SetKwFunction{Span}{SpanCorridor}
\SetKwFunction{Leaf}{Leaf}
\SetKwFunction{NodeOf}{CutNode}
\SetKwFunction{NewNode}{NewNode}
\SetKwFunction{CellAt}{CellAt}
\SetKwFunction{WithAxis}{WithAxis}
\KwIn{$W,H\ge1$, the width and height of the area in cells. The bound is a
      precondition, not a convention: at $W=0$ the region $\region{0,0}{-1,H-1}$
      holds no cell and there is nothing for the first draw to land on.}
\KwOut{$N$, the set of nodes holding a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$; $r$, the
       root of the region tree that built it.}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\GenerateMaze{$W$, $H$}}{
  $N \leftarrow \{\,\NewNode{$x$, $y$} : 0\le x<W,\; 0\le y<H\,\}$
     \tcp*{every neighbour list empty}
  $r \leftarrow$ \Divide{$N$, $(0,0)$, $(W-1,H-1)$}\;
  \Return $(N,r)$\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\Divide{$N$, $p$, $q$}}{
  \lIf{$p[0]=q[0]$ \textup{\textbf{or}} $p[1]=q[1]$}{\Return \Span{$N$, $p$, $q$}}
  $t \leftarrow$ \RandomBit{}\tcp*{the axis to cut: $0$ is $x$, $1$ is $y$}
  $j \leftarrow$ \RandomIn{$p[t]$, $q[t]-1$}\tcp*{wall between $j$ and $j+1$ on axis $t$}
  $i \leftarrow$ \RandomIn{$p[1{-}t]$, $q[1{-}t]$}\tcp*{the one door, at $i$ on the other axis}
  \Link{$N$, \CellAt{$t$, $j$, $i$}, \CellAt{$t$, $j{+}1$, $i$}}\;
  $\ell \leftarrow$ \Divide{$N$, $p$, \WithAxis{$q$, $t$, $j$}}\;
  $r \leftarrow$ \Divide{$N$, \WithAxis{$p$, $t$, $j{+}1$}, $q$}\;
  \Return \NodeOf{$t$, $j$, $i$, $\ell$, $r$}\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\Span{$N$, $p$, $q$}}{
  \lFor{$x \leftarrow p[0]$ \KwTo $q[0]-1$}{\Link{$N$, $(x,p[1])$, $(x{+}1,p[1])$}}
  \lFor{$y \leftarrow p[1]$ \KwTo $q[1]-1$}{\Link{$N$, $(p[0],y)$, $(p[0],y{+}1)$}}
  \Return \Leaf{$p$, $q$}\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\Link{$N$, $u$, $v$}}{
  append $N[v]$ to $N[u].\textit{neighbours}$\;
  append $N[u]$ to $N[v].\textit{neighbours}$\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\CellAt{$t$, $u$, $v$}}{
  \Return the cell whose axis-$t$ coordinate is $u$ and whose other is $v$\;
}
\BlankLine
\Fn{\WithAxis{$p$, $t$, $u$}}{
  \Return the cell $p$ with its axis-$t$ coordinate replaced by $u$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\Span{} is the whole of the base case: it lays the corridor's forced doors
\emph{and} returns the leaf that records the region, so what used to be two
lines that had to be kept in step is one call. It needs no case analysis
either --- on a corridor one of its loops is empty by construction and on a
single cell both are, so the same two loops cover a row, a column and one cell.

\CellAt{} and \WithAxis{} are the only price of indexing the axis, one line
each, and they are what let the division be written once rather than twice.

Every \Link{} joins two cells of the region that called it, and they are
adjacent --- so every appended entry is a genuine edge of $\gridgraph$.

The exit plays no part in generation, for the reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:exit-constraints}: the algorithm never reads it, and any cell
at all may be designated once the tree exists.

\subsection{Why the result is a spanning tree}

\begin{proposition}[Correctness of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generate-maze}]
\label{prop:generation-is-a-tree}
On a region of $m$ cells, \textup{\texttt{Divide}} leaves the subgraph induced
on that region connected and acyclic, with exactly $m-1$ doors. In particular
\textup{\texttt{GenerateMaze}} returns a spanning tree of $\gridgraph$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $m$. If the region has a side of one it is a corridor of $m$
cells in a line, and \texttt{SpanCorridor} joins each to the next: a path, which
is connected and acyclic and carries $m-1$ doors. This covers $m=1$ too, where
both loops are empty and $m-1=0$.

Otherwise both sides are at least $2$, so $q[0]-p[0]\ge1$ and $q[1]-p[1]\ge1$:
whichever axis the coin names, $q[t]-p[t]\ge1$, so $j$ is drawn from a non-empty
range and the two sub-regions $R_\ell$ and $R_r$ are non-empty, with
$m_\ell+m_r=m$. The call adds
the single door at $i$, whose two cells lie one in each sub-region, and then
recurses; by the induction hypothesis the two calls leave their regions
connected and acyclic with $m_\ell-1$ and $m_r-1$ doors. Neither recursive call
can add an edge between the two sub-regions, because every \texttt{Link} a call
makes joins two cells of its own region.

So the door at $i$ is the only edge between $R_\ell$ and $R_r$. Joining two
disjoint trees by a single edge yields a tree: connected, since any two cells
are joined inside their own half or by crossing that door; acyclic, since a
cycle would have to cross the wall an even, hence non-zero, number of times
while only one crossing exists. The door count is
$(m_\ell-1)+(m_r-1)+1=m-1$.

Applying this to the whole area gives a connected acyclic spanning subgraph of
$\gridgraph$ on all $WH$ cells: a spanning tree, with $WH-1$ doors.
\end{proof}

The guarantee is therefore structural. Nothing is checked after the fact, no
attempt is ever rejected and retried, and the uniqueness of the route out ---
the whole of Problem~2 --- is a consequence of the shape of the recursion
rather than of a property that had to be tested.

\subsection{The graph that comes out}

Generation returns the set $N$ of nodes, each carrying its coordinates and the
doors it ended up with, together with the root $r$ of the region tree. Three
readings of that one result, none of them a separate copy:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  View & Held as & Used by \\
  \midrule
  Adjacency          & each node's neighbour list
                     & the search of Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} \\
  Position           & each node's \textit{value}
                     & the heuristics of Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} \\
  How it was built   & the region tree rooted at $r$
                     & the cost analysis of Section~\ref{sec:analysis} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The second row is what makes Section~\ref{sec:shortest-path} possible at all: a
heuristic that estimates the remaining distance to the exit needs the
coordinates of the cell it is standing on, and they are in the node.

Figure~\ref{fig:maze-as-graph} is the output of one $4\times4$ run, whose first
three divisions are drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:region-tree-cuts}: $15$ doors on
$16$ cells, one path between any two of them. What
Section~\ref{sec:unit-edge-weights} adds is a length on those doors, so that
``the path out'' becomes a quantity a search can minimise.
